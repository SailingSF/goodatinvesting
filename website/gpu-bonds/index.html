How to borrow billions for GPUs

...and what you actually own when you lend it to them

Published: November 6, 2026

Borrowing money as a big company

Almost every company borrows money. When you're big enough, you can just say "I need $100 billion, I'll pay it back in 2030." If you're Apple, the market goes "Ok, but you gotta pay us $120 billion in 2030." If you're CoreWeave, it's "You gotta pay us $175 billion." These promises to pay back money are bonds, and the difference in what each company has to pay back is the yield.

Why does fixed income matter?

The AI buildout is the largest project by value we've seen in our lifetimes, and even if you're not going to buy one of these bonds in your brokerage account they represent hundreds of billions of dollars already owned by pension funds, life insurers, and governments around the world. It's helpful to know what these things are that have soaked up traditionally "risk averse" dollars of the bond market. You probably own some already through a bond fund or a pension.

And if the AI buildout goes wrong, it goes wrong through credit: the lenders decide who gets refinanced and who doesn't. The bonds also tell you what the market thinks the buildout is worth in a way the stock can't.

The risk ladder

This post walks through how different companies are financing the AI buildout, what their lenders actually own, and how to analyze it.

The bonds we'll look at are a ladder of risk and I'll show what investors get paid and what they are entitled to other than a pay day at the end of the term. My risk ladder is Apple (the control) → Google (the hyperscaler with less debt) → Meta (the new-er hyperscaler with some funky ways to not record debt on their balance sheet) → SpaceX (the newest hyperscaler who's building a lot of datacenters) → and CoreWeave (the neocloud who rents buildings to put GPUs in and then sells their usage).

Meta has two additional vehicles that issue the debt for their AI datacenters called Beignet and Sopaipilla who borrow the money and charge rent to Meta to pay back the money. I'll cover exactly what those are later.

A note on dates: every bond price is a Nov 10, 2026 TRACE last trade, and every option price is the Nov 9 close (CoreWeave's stock was ~$49, SpaceX's ~$142).

Meet the Bonds

These bonds are all rated and then priced by the open market. Here is a look at the spread (in basis points over Treasuries) of all the relevant bonds I looked at compared to the average of their given rating.

The control(s)

Apple is not a very indebted company, they aren't building out AI datacenters, and they print money. You can't be more safe than AAPL.

Alphabet (read: Google) is building a lot of datacenters, but they make a lot of money. They were supposed to be the one that funded AI by selling stock instead of borrowing. They did both: $51.8B of bonds in the first half of this year and an $84.75B "equity raise." Here's the quiet part: about $30B of that raise is a paperwork change to how Google pays its employees' stock-comp taxes. And they still only pay 0.7–1.2% over Treasuries to borrow.

Both of these companies could repay their debt in under a year if they decided to stop growing.

Meta - Facebook and Instagram's cash flows

What do you own with a Meta bond? A senior unsecured claim on Facebook and Instagram's cash flows. Why does it pay slightly more? It's longer dated and there is a lot of competition for these AI bonds, meaning more supply, meaning more yield. Or the market thinks Meta is a little more likely to go bankrupt than Google and wants an extra ~0.25% a year for it. The stock says the odds are about zero, so it's not that.

Beignet and Sopaipilla - Meta's Landlord

Beignet and Sopaipilla are two "Special Purpose Vehicles" (SPVs) that Meta set up with Blue Owl (Beignet, the Hyperion campus in Louisiana) and BlackRock (‼️) (Sopaipilla, in El Paso, Texas). Each SPV owns 80% of a datacenter campus (Meta owns the other 20%), issues bonds to pay for building it, and leases it to Meta, whose rent pays the bonds back. Your collateral is that 80% stake in the campus, not GPUs.

Meta has a 4-year lease on these datacenters, with the expectation to renew for up to 20 years. But they can walk away. If they do, and the SPV has no other way to make money, the campus gets sold. Meta covers some of the losses through a "residual value guarantee," but that guarantee is paid to the joint venture, not to you, and it shrinks over time.

These bonds only lose if Meta walks away and the guarantee doesn't cover the gap. You're paid ~1.1–1.25% a year more than Meta's own bonds (~2–2.5% over Treasuries) for being short Meta's option to leave, plus for being stuck in a bond that's hard to sell. If you think Meta has a reasonable chance of giving up on AI in 4 years and taking the reputation hit, don't buy this bond.

Now the juicier ones.

SpaceX - The IPO wasn't enough. Elon Needs More

You thought this company was about rockets? You thought $75 billion was enough? Wrong. It's about chips and they've already issued another $25 billion with a need for another $190-455 billion in the future according to analysts and their own words.

All of this debt means we finally get some real yield. SpaceX is rated BBB, the last rung before junk, but its bonds already pay more than the average junk bond (~255bp over Treasuries vs. ~200bp for BB). The market isn't waiting for the rating agencies. At 7.2% for the 2031 bonds, investors are paid well above Treasuries to fund this massive hyperscaler build-out, backed up by a profitable space-based internet business... and Elon.

SpaceX's business is increasingly selling compute rather than using it for their own model which means the hope is that they can provide compute into a scarce market and continue to charge high prices, further funding more datacenters. By owning SpaceX bonds you are betting that compute will remain scarce and Elon will be able to find new money all while backed up by a stock worth trillions and a pretty good space business.

Here's the quiet part: nothing in your bond ties Starlink or the ~$1.9 trillion stock to you. There are no subsidiary guarantees, and the covenants let SpaceX pledge new GPUs to new lenders ahead of you. The first ~$40B, a lease from Apollo, already does: Apollo owns those GPUs, so it gets paid first. You're the first mortgage on a house whose owner is about to take out several bigger loans on the extensions. That's why the stock sees ~1bp of default risk while the bonds pay ~255bp. You aren't paid for SpaceX going broke, you're paid for where you'll stand in line if it does. And the line starts growing in 2027, when SpaceX needs new money, years before your bond comes due.

But if SpaceX stopped building new datacenters and just used the ones they have, they would be a-ok. It just seems like borrowing money can be addictive. Speaking of addicted to borrowing money...

CoreWeave - Stumbling into the hottest business of all time

The story of CoreWeave is a funny one. A company that started as a GPU cloud for rendering videos (back when that's what GPUs were for) found that mining Ethereum during idle time was pretty profitable, and then AI came around and it turns out their crypto miners and relationship with Nvidia was worth a lot. So they borrowed a ton of money to buy GPUs, signed long leases on buildings to put them in, and sell time on those GPUs under contracts that end years before the leases do.

Contracts average about 5 years, and most of today's backlog is used up by mid-2030, but the rent runs to ~2039. From 2031, ~$45B of rent is due against only ~$21B of contracted revenue. It's a restaurant with a 13-year lease and customers who only book a few years at a time.

CoreWeave's corporate bonds yield a whole 15% for their 2031 notes. That much extra yield might tell you something is wrong, and there might be. CoreWeave's corporate bonds are held up by CoreWeave's ability to keep doing business and they have some great and secure customers. Their largest customer is Meta, who will pay them up to $35 billion over the next 5 years. But didn't we just talk about how Meta is financing their own datacenters? Oh yeah. So if Meta even slightly retreats from AI (or just doesn't grow as exponentially as they have) CoreWeave could be looking at a big hole in their revenue.

A lot of their customers are sourcing compute elsewhere which means there is competition and if you stop being the monopolist on GPUs you lose your pricing power. When a 5-year contract ends, lenders are only made whole if that old GPU keeps earning at least 46% of what it earned under contract, which means hourly rates on old GPUs can fall no faster than ~14% a year. For a while that looked bad. H100 rents fell ~80% from their 2023 shortage price to ~$1.50–2.00 an hour by early 2026, about 19% a year, which is the curve I used in my SpaceX post. Then they turned around. Today an H100 rents for ~$2.50 an hour spot and ~$3.10 in executed trades, so measured from Jan 2024 the price is roughly flat, comfortably inside the ~14% a year lenders need. One good year isn't a trend, but it isn't the slide I expected either.

The "glut" line on this chart is the path H100s were on before 2026. The 2026 rebound puts today's market closer to the "shortage lasts" line.

The problem is that the corporate bondholders aren't entitled to any of the revenue from the big contracts, those all went to secured lenders and yield much less. These higher yielding bonds are a bet that CoreWeave has plenty left over even after paying back the first lenders.

What else can CoreWeave fall back on? They don't have a cute little side business of launching and landing rockets and selling satellite internet. They could sell more of their stock. Diluting shareholders in order to pay back their debt is a real backstop but there's only so much you can do and the stock is hoping for continued pricing power in GPUs; so if GPU rents keep falling then the stock keeps falling and there's not much left to sell to pay back bond holders.

Then CoreWeave's Q3 numbers came out on Nov 10, and they change the story. One thing to keep straight: the GPU loans are borrowed by CoreWeave's subsidiaries and paid from their contracts first. Your bond is a claim on the parent, CoreWeave, Inc., which only gets what's left over.

CoreWeave's old contracts were priced to pay their GPU loans and almost nothing else. Of every revenue dollar, about 11¢ makes it to the parent after the lender. On the ~$104B of backlog it had signed by June, that's ~$10B over the next seven years, against ~$32B the parent owes: $21B of its own bonds and convertibles due 2030–33, plus interest and the equity it still has to put into data centers being built. That's the razor's edge: on the old book alone, the cash runs out in 2031.

Two things get it off the edge. Since July, CoreWeave has been signing at $20–25M per megawatt a year instead of ~$9M, so more than half of each new dollar survives the loan: Q3's contracts alone leave ~$19B. And when a contract ends, the GPUs are paid off and CoreWeave owns them outright: renting them at what an old A100 fetches today adds ~$27B by 2033. Add those in and CoreWeave ends 2033 with ~$29B, or ~$16B even if old-GPU rents fall back to their 2026 low.

The bonds are fine if CoreWeave stops here. The risk is that it doesn't: it plans to spend ~$38B again next year, and if it signs that round at old prices, it's back on the edge.

At ~15%, you're paid fairly for a real risk, but it's a narrower risk than "GPUs stay scarce through 2031." What CoreWeave has already signed, plus its GPUs' second life, pays your bonds if it stops here. The bet is on how it signs the next ~$38B. If it keeps signing at today's prices, it's a good bet. If the shortage ends and it signs at old-book prices, it's back on the edge, and selling stock won't save it, because the stock is riding the same bet. If you do buy, own the 2030s (~14.6%, due June 2030): they get paid back before 2031, when the rent starts to outrun the old contracts, and while the stock is most likely still worth selling. And watch what the 2027 contracts get priced at, and what a 5-year-old GPU rents for when the old contracts roll off in 2028–30.

So what is the yield paying for?

Put it all together and here's the chart I wish I'd seen before I started: each bond's spread, split into the default risk the stock market sees and everything else.

For Apple, Alphabet and Meta it's ~all "everything else": being long-dated and the flood of new supply. For Beignet it's the structure, not Meta. For SpaceX it's ~255bp, of which the stock sees ~1bp: you're paid for getting pushed down the line. Only CoreWeave's yield is mostly paying for default, and there the stock and the bonds agree on how big the risk is, just not on when it shows up.

Once you know what each yield pays for, you can bet on the piece you disagree with.

How do I make money on this? (I really want to)

Here are some trading ideas I came up with to juice the yield a bit. If you've never traded options, read the primer first. If you have, skip to the table.

A 60-second primer on options

  • A call is the right to buy a stock at a set price (the strike) until a set date (the expiry). It pays off when the stock goes up. A $90 call on CoreWeave is worth $10 a share at expiry if CRWV ends at $100, and nothing if it ends below $90.
  • A put is the right to sell a stock at the strike. It pays off when the stock goes down. A $20 put is worth $5 a share if CRWV ends at $15, and nothing if it ends above $20.
  • Buying an option costs you money up front (the premium), and the most you can lose is what you paid. Selling one pays you the premium up front, and in exchange you owe the buyer whatever it's worth at expiry. Selling is how you get paid to take a risk, which is exactly what a bondholder does.
  • A spread is two options of the same type at different strikes: you buy one and sell the other. It caps both what you can make and what you can lose. When you sell a $20/$10 put spread, you sell the $20 put and buy the $10 put. You collect cash today and owe at most $10 a share ($20 − $10) if the stock ends below $10.
  • One contract covers 100 shares, so a $10-wide spread can cost you at most $1,000.
  • Every option here expires Jan 19, 2029, the longest-dated ones listed.

Every trade below says which kind it uses. A put spread bets on (or insures against) the stock going down. A call spread bets on it going up.

And a quick note on using bonds in a trade: buying $10,000 of a bond's face value means you pay today's price (85.50¢ on the dollar for CoreWeave's 2030s, so $8,550), collect the coupons, and get $10,000 back at maturity if the company pays. You don't have to hold it that long. You can sell it any time at the market price, which falls when the company looks riskier and rises when it looks safer. Every trade here is priced as if you sell the bond in Jan 2029, at today's yield, when the options expire.

Build your own bond

Every risky bond is really two things glued together: a safe loan to the government, plus an insurance policy you've sold against the company going bust. The Treasury part pays ~4.6%. The insurance part pays the rest: CoreWeave's ~14.6% minus 4.6% leaves ~10% a year for taking the hit if CoreWeave can't pay.

You can't buy CoreWeave's bond (it's 144A), but you can buy the two halves separately:

  • The safe loan: buy Treasuries maturing around Jan 2029 with the same cash the bond would cost. They pay ~4.6% a year and sit in your account as collateral for the options.
  • The insurance: sell put spreads on the stock. A sold put spread pays you cash today and only costs you money if the stock ends below the strike you sold, which is roughly what happens to a stock when a company heads toward default. Like a bond, you collect a fixed amount if things go fine and take a capped loss if they don't. Buying the lower put is what caps the loss, the same way a bond can't lose more than you paid for it.

For CoreWeave, put ~$8,550 in Treasuries and sell seven $20/$10 put spreads: sell seven $20 puts and buy seven $10 puts. You collect $2,170 up front. The most you could ever owe is $7,000 (seven spreads × $10 × 100 shares), which your Treasuries cover. If CoreWeave's stock is above $20 in Jan 2029, both sets of puts expire worthless and you don't owe anything, and your credit from the options plus Treasuries earns +$3,050, 14.9% a year vs. 14.0% for the bond held to the same date. You break even down to ~$16, and the most you can lose is ~$3,950 (the bond loses ~$2,500 in a default). The catch: the options market gives CoreWeave ~44% odds of ending below $16, so this is a bet on the stock, not just on default.

For SpaceX you have to be a bit more aggressive with your options, because the market is really not pricing in a default for SpaceX. But if you thought SpaceX wasn't going to fall much you can beat the bond's yield by putting ~$9,274 in Treasuries and selling two $60/$30 put spreads (sell two $60 puts, buy two $30 puts) for $806. If SpaceX is above $60 at expiry you don't owe anything, and with the Treasuries you make +$1,761, 8.2% a year vs. 7.0% for the bond held to the same date. You break even at ~$51 (a 64% fall, ~22% odds per the options market) and lose up to ~$4,240 below $30. That's stock risk, not default risk: the deep put spreads that only lose in a real default pay almost nothing on SpaceX.

Another disclaimer: "building your own bond" has a significant margin impact, and your Treasury purchase may not be as liquid as your options if you hold the Treasury notes directly. If you use ETFs you pay even more fees.

CoreWeave bonds are fine, their stock is not

This trade is if you think CoreWeave will be able to pay back their debt, but by diluting shareholders and issuing stock. So we can get that ~15% yield but we think their stock will fall by issuing more shares or investors losing faith in a more competitive market.

Trade 1: Dilution, not default

Buy the June 2030 notes (14.6%), sell a January 2029 $90 call (just above Nvidia's $87.20 investment), and buy a $20 put. Selling the call is the "the stock won't take off" half. Buying the put is insurance. The call brings in $14.45 a share ($1,445) and the put costs $6.95 ($695), so the call pays for your insurance with $750 to spare. The put means you still come out slightly ahead in a default. The call means you give up the upside if the stock takes off, and since it isn't capped, your loss is technically infinite as the stock keeps rising. A "dilution rescue" here means no default, but CoreWeave sells enough stock to pay its debts and the price falls to $30.

  • Dilution rescue ($30): +$4,013, 19.2% a year (bond alone +$3,263, 15.9%)
  • Nothing changes: +$3,586, 17.3% a year (bond alone +$2,837, 14.0%)
  • Default: +$131, 0.7% a year (bond alone −$2,519, −14.7%)

The "free money" goes away if the stock rises above ~$126 (the options market gives that ~13% odds), if CoreWeave defaults after the put expires in Jan 2029, or if in a default CoreWeave recovers less than 40% of the money owed.

Trade 2: Complicated just for the fun of it

If you want to have a fun options trade and think that CoreWeave isn't going to default but renting GPUs isn't going to take them to the moon, you can create an iron condor and buy some Treasuries, to sell the upside (and the downside) and make some yield. An iron condor is just a sold put spread and a sold call spread at the same time: you get paid as long as the stock ends up between them. Put the ~$8,225 in Treasuries, sell ten $10/$5 put spreads (sell the $10 puts, buy the $5 puts; that's the "default" part of the bond) and sell two $90/$135 call spreads (sell the $90 calls, buy the $135 calls; that's "selling the upside"). You collect $1,800 up front.

  • Anywhere between ~$10 and $90 in Jan 2029: +$2,680, 13.2% a year, no 144A bond required.
  • You break even at ~$7 and ~$103.
  • Default: −$2,320. The shortage lasts ($120): −$3,320. The most you can lose is ~$6,300, if CoreWeave ends above $135.

It's less than the bond's ~15%, but every loss is capped and you don't have to be an institution.

SpaceX - Solvent but overvalued

These are the trades if you're like me and think SpaceX is not going bankrupt in the near future but also they are definitely overvalued. Read my last post to see my SpaceX valuation.

Trade 3: The SpaceX seagull (with an extra wing)

A seagull usually has three legs. This one has four. Buy Treasuries, buy a $75/$25 put spread (buy the $75 put, sell the $25 put), and sell a $250/$295 call spread (sell the $250 call, buy the $295 call). The put spread pays off if SpaceX falls. The call spread you sold helps pay for it, and costs you if SpaceX soars. The options cost $460, which about half of the Treasury interest ($955) pays for. +$5,418 (22.3% a year) if SpaceX falls to a $350B valuation (~$26 a share), +$495 if nothing changes (2.3% a year, so you've swapped about half your Treasury interest for a lottery ticket on my valuation), and a maximum loss of ~$4,000 if SpaceX ends above $295 and becomes the hottest compute seller of all time. You lose money above ~$255.

Trade 4: Let the bond buy you a put

Buy the SpaceX notes and a $100 put ($1,760). If you're not an institution, buy Treasuries and a $100/$25 put spread instead (buy the $100 put, sell the $25 put) for $1,680. Either way you're spending some of the bond's interest on insurance against SpaceX falling.

These trades don't lose more than ~$725 (−3.1% a year), and if the stock falls all the way to a $350B valuation we make ~$6,700–7,100 (24–25% a year). The Treasury version does better in a real default (+$6,800 vs. +$3,600), because Treasuries don't default.

Trade 5: Institutional "nothing ever happens"

If you're an institution and can buy their bond, sell a $200 call against it ($3,080) and suddenly their bond's 7.0% a year (held to Jan 2029) turns into 20.0% a year if nothing changes. It only loses money if SpaceX goes above ~$246 (~17% odds), but above that the loss is unlimited: at $300 it's −$5,200.

SpaceX Bull

There is no way you're a SpaceX bull if you've made it this far with me, but here's a little treat in case you are.

Trade 6: The homemade convertible bond spread

Buy ~$9,274 of Treasuries and spend another ~$3,075 (about a third as much again) to buy a $150/$295 call spread (buy the $150 call, sell the $295 call). This is essentially a convertible bond where your note turns into shares, and if SpaceX goes up this spread will pay out up to $14,500 at $295 or above, +$12,380 net (37.2% a year), essentially turning your 4.6% yielding Treasury into a leveraged bull play on SpaceX. The only downside is having to pay for this up front, unlike a convertible note. Oh, and if SpaceX isn't above ~$171 at expiry you lose money, at most $2,120 (−8.2% a year).

So what do you actually own?

  • Apple, Alphabet, Meta: the company. You're paid for lending long into a flood of new bonds, not for default.
  • Beignet and Sopaipilla: a loan to Meta's landlord. You're short Meta's option to leave.
  • SpaceX: an unsecured promise that's about to get pushed down the line. You're paid for where you'll stand, not for whether SpaceX survives.
  • CoreWeave: a bet that the next round of contracts gets signed at today's prices, not the old ones. What's already signed, plus the GPUs' second life, covers you if CoreWeave stops here. You're paid for real default risk, and it may show up sooner than the bonds think.

What I'm watching:

  • What CoreWeave's 2027 contracts get priced at: ~$20–25M per megawatt like Q3's, or back toward the old book's ~$9M.
  • How the Apollo lease gets priced (expected at year-end), the first big loan to cut in line ahead of SpaceX's bondholders.
  • Who Meta's guarantee actually pays. The offering documents aren't public, and Meta's 10-Q (paid to the joint venture) and the press (sized so the bonds are repaid) don't agree.
  • What a Blackwell GPU rents for in two years. H100 rents turned around in 2026, but one good year isn't a trend, and newer chips may hold their value differently.

The 144A bonds trade thinly, so prices are single prints from one day. None of this is investment advice.
